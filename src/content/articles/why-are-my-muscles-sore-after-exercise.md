You try a new class, go hard on leg day or hike down a mountain. You feel fine that evening — then the next morning, stairs become your enemy. By the second day it's even worse. This delayed ache has a name: **delayed onset muscle soreness**, or **DOMS**. And despite what many people still believe, lactic acid has nothing to do with it.

> **In short:** DOMS is caused by **microscopic damage to muscle fibers** — especially from **eccentric** (lengthening) movements like lowering weights or running downhill — plus the **inflammatory repair process** that follows. It starts 12–24 hours after exercise, peaks at 24–72 hours and fades within about a week.

## The lactic acid myth

For decades, gym wisdom blamed next-day soreness on lactic acid "building up" in your muscles. It's an appealing story, but the evidence doesn't support it:

- **Lactate clears fast.** Studies show lactate levels return to normal within about **an hour** after exercise — long before DOMS appears.
- **No correlation.** Researchers have found little relationship between lactate levels right after exercise and the soreness people feel days later.
- **The wrong exercises cause it.** Concentric (shortening) exercise produces plenty of lactate yet causes little soreness, while eccentric exercise produces less lactate but much more soreness.

That burning feeling *during* hard exercise is a different phenomenon. DOMS is a delayed, separate process.

## What actually causes DOMS

The leading explanation involves two linked steps.

### 1. Microscopic muscle damage

Muscles contract in three basic ways:

| Contraction type | What happens | Everyday example | DOMS risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Concentric** | Muscle shortens while producing force | Lifting a dumbbell up in a curl | Low |
| **Isometric** | Muscle produces force without changing length | Holding a plank | Moderate |
| **Eccentric** | Muscle lengthens while producing force | Lowering the dumbbell slowly; running downhill; walking down stairs | **High** |

Eccentric contractions put muscle fibers under high tension while they're being stretched. This causes tiny disruptions in the fibers' contractile units (sarcomeres) and in surrounding connective tissue. It isn't a "tear" in the injury sense — it's microscopic.

### 2. The repair response

Your body responds to that micro-damage the way it responds to many minor injuries: with **inflammation**. Immune cells move in, fluid accumulates, and chemical signals sensitize nearby nerve endings. That sensitization is why sore muscles feel tender to the touch and painful when stretched. The process takes time to ramp up — which explains the "delayed" in DOMS.

## The DOMS timeline

- **0–12 hours:** Usually little or no soreness.
- **12–24 hours:** Stiffness and tenderness begin.
- **24–72 hours:** Peak soreness, often with temporary loss of strength and range of motion.
- **5–7 days:** Soreness generally resolves.

## Who gets sore — and why it gets easier

DOMS hits hardest when a workout is **new, more intense than usual, or heavy on eccentric movement**. That's why beginners, people returning after a break, and anyone trying an unfamiliar sport tend to suffer most.

The good news is the **repeated bout effect**: after one session of a particular exercise, your muscles adapt so that repeating it causes much less soreness, sometimes for weeks afterward. This is your body becoming more resilient — not a sign you've stopped making progress.

## Does soreness mean a better workout?

No. Soreness tells you a workout was **unfamiliar**, not that it was effective. Experienced athletes often train hard without much soreness at all. Chasing soreness as a goal can lead to overdoing it and interrupting your training consistency, which matters far more for results.

## What helps with recovery

There's no instant cure, but some approaches can ease discomfort:

- **Gentle movement.** Light walking (one of the easiest everyday habits — see [what happens when you walk every day](/articles/what-happens-when-you-walk-every-day/)), cycling or easy swimming increases blood flow and often reduces stiffness temporarily.
- **Time.** DOMS resolves on its own; patience is the most reliable remedy.
- **Massage.** Some research suggests massage after exercise can modestly reduce soreness.
- **Sleep and nutrition.** Adequate sleep, protein and overall energy intake support the repair process.
- **Heat or cold.** Many people find warm baths or cold packs soothing, though evidence for big effects is mixed.

## How to prevent excessive soreness

- **Progress gradually.** Increase weight, distance or intensity a little at a time.
- **Ease into eccentric-heavy activities** like downhill running, hiking descents or slow lowering phases.
- **Repeat new exercises** — the second session usually hurts far less than the first.
- **Warm up** before demanding sessions.

## DOMS vs. injury: how to tell the difference

It's useful to recognize what ordinary soreness feels like so you can spot when something is different:

| | Typical DOMS | Possible injury |
|---|---|---|
| **When it starts** | 12–24 hours after exercise | Suddenly, during the activity |
| **How it feels** | Dull, diffuse ache and stiffness | Sharp, stabbing or localized pain |
| **Where** | Across the worked muscles, often both sides | One specific spot, or in a joint |
| **With movement** | Often eases as you warm up | Gets worse with use |
| **Duration** | Improves steadily within about a week | Persists or worsens |

If your pain matches the right-hand column, rest the area and consider getting it checked rather than training through it.

## When soreness isn't normal

DOMS is uncomfortable but harmless. Contact a healthcare professional if you have:

- Pain that is **severe or out of proportion** to the exercise you did
- **Significant swelling**, or a limb that feels extremely weak
- **Dark, tea- or cola-colored urine**

These can be signs of **rhabdomyolysis**, a rare but serious condition in which damaged muscle releases substances that can harm the kidneys. Sharp pain during exercise, or pain in a joint rather than a muscle, may also indicate an injury rather than DOMS.

## The takeaway

The soreness that peaks a day or two after a hard workout is DOMS: microscopic muscle damage from eccentric movement, followed by an inflammatory repair process. Lactic acid is not to blame. Soreness fades on its own within a week, gets much milder as your body adapts, and can be kept in check by progressing gradually.
