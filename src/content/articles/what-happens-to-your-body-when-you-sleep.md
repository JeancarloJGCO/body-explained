You spend roughly a third of your life asleep. From the outside it looks like nothing is happening — you're lying still with your eyes closed. Inside, however, your brain and body are running a carefully choreographed program: repairing tissue, releasing hormones, sorting memories and cycling through very different states of activity.

> **In short:** Sleep has two main types — **non-REM** (three stages, N1 to N3) and **REM** (rapid eye movement). You move through all four stages in cycles of about 90 minutes, repeating them four to six times a night. Deep sleep dominates early in the night; REM sleep grows longer toward morning.

## Two kinds of sleep

Scientists divide sleep into two fundamentally different states:

- **Non-REM (NREM) sleep** — the brain gradually slows down, moving from light to very deep sleep. It's split into three stages: N1, N2 and N3.
- **REM sleep** — the brain becomes highly active, the eyes dart rapidly behind closed lids, and most vivid dreaming happens.

You might see older sources describe *four* non-REM stages. Sleep specialists merged the old stages 3 and 4 into a single stage (N3) in 2007 because there was no meaningful physiological difference between them.

## Stage N1: drifting off

N1 is the doorway between wakefulness and sleep — often announced by a few big yawns (here's [why we yawn](/articles/why-do-we-yawn/)). It usually lasts only **one to five minutes**.

- Your heartbeat, breathing and eye movements start to slow.
- Muscles relax, though they may twitch.
- Brain waves begin to slow from their daytime patterns.

N1 is very light — a noise can easily wake you, and you might not even realize you'd fallen asleep. That sudden "falling" sensation followed by a jerk (a *hypnic jerk*) often happens here.

## Stage N2: light but real sleep

In N2 you're properly asleep, but not deeply.

- Heart rate and breathing slow further.
- Body temperature drops.
- Eye movements stop.
- Brain waves slow, interrupted by brief bursts of electrical activity called **sleep spindles**.

You spend **more total time in N2 than in any other stage** — roughly half of a typical night. Researchers believe sleep spindles play a role in processing and stabilizing memories.

## Stage N3: deep, restorative sleep

N3 — often called **deep sleep** or **slow-wave sleep** — is the most restorative stage.

- Heartbeat and breathing reach their slowest, most regular rhythm.
- Muscles are fully relaxed.
- Brain waves become slow and large (delta waves).
- It is **hard to wake someone** from N3, and people woken from it often feel disoriented.

This is when much of the body's physical maintenance happens: tissue repair, growth (growth hormone is released largely during deep sleep, which is especially important for children) and support for the immune system. Deep sleep is concentrated in the **first half of the night** and becomes shorter in later cycles.

## REM sleep: the active brain

About 90 minutes after you fall asleep, you enter your first REM period. REM is strikingly different from the stages before it.

- **Eyes move rapidly** side to side behind closed eyelids.
- **Brain activity rises** to levels close to wakefulness.
- **Breathing and heart rate become faster and irregular.**
- **Most voluntary muscles are temporarily paralyzed**, which prevents you from physically acting out your dreams.
- The **amygdala**, a brain region involved in emotion, becomes especially active.

REM is where the most vivid, story-like dreams happen, although dreaming can occur in other stages too. It's thought to be important for learning, memory and emotional processing. In adults, REM makes up roughly **20–25% of total sleep**. The first REM period may last only a few minutes, but later ones can stretch to an hour.

## How the cycles fit together

| Stage | Type | Depth | Key features | Typical share of night |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| N1 | Non-REM | Very light | Transition to sleep, muscle twitches | ~5% |
| N2 | Non-REM | Light | Sleep spindles, temperature drops | ~45–50% |
| N3 | Non-REM | Deep | Slow delta waves, physical repair | ~15–25% |
| REM | REM | Active brain | Rapid eye movements, vivid dreams, muscle paralysis | ~20–25% |

*Percentages are approximate for healthy adults and vary with age and individual sleep patterns.*

A typical night looks something like this: N1 → N2 → N3 → N2 → REM, then the cycle starts again. Each cycle lasts about **90 minutes**, and most adults go through **four to six cycles** per night. Early cycles are rich in deep N3 sleep; later cycles contain more REM. That's one reason cutting your sleep short in the morning tends to rob you of REM in particular.

## What else changes while you sleep

Beyond the stages themselves, a lot of whole-body housekeeping happens overnight:

- **Hormones:** Growth hormone peaks during deep sleep. Hormones that regulate appetite are also influenced by sleep, which helps explain why poor sleep can affect hunger.
- **Memory:** Pathways involved in learning become more active, helping consolidate what you learned during the day.
- **Body temperature:** Your core temperature dips, reaching its lowest point in the early morning hours.
- **The brain's cleanup:** Research suggests sleep helps the brain clear out waste products that build up during waking hours.

## Why this matters for you

Understanding the cycle can make your sleep habits more intuitive. Consistent bedtimes help your body predict when to start each stage. Getting a full night protects both the deep sleep of the early hours and the REM-rich hours before dawn. And if you regularly wake up groggy, you may be getting pulled out of deep sleep — something a more regular schedule can help with.

If you consistently feel unrested despite adequate time in bed, snore loudly, or notice pauses in your breathing during sleep, it's worth talking to a healthcare professional, as these can be signs of a sleep disorder.

## The takeaway

Sleep is an active, structured process. Your brain moves through light N1 and N2 sleep, deep restorative N3 sleep and the vivid, brain-busy REM stage, repeating the loop roughly every 90 minutes. Each stage contributes something different — and getting enough complete cycles is what leaves you feeling truly rested.
