You're sitting in a warm room during a long meeting, and suddenly your jaw drops open in a huge, uncontrollable yawn. A second later, the person across the table does the same. Yawning is one of the most familiar things the body does, yet scientists are still debating exactly why it happens — and why it spreads from person to person so easily.

> **In short:** Nobody knows for certain why we yawn, but the leading ideas are that a yawn helps the brain stay alert and may help cool it down. Contagious yawning seems to be linked to social connection: people catch yawns most easily from those they feel close to.

## What actually happens during a yawn

A yawn is more than a big breath. It's a coordinated movement that involves your jaw, face, throat, chest and diaphragm all at once. A typical yawn lasts about **4 to 7 seconds** and unfolds in three phases:

1. **The long inhale.** Your mouth opens wide, your jaw stretches, and you draw in a deep breath. The airway at the back of the throat widens and the muscles of the face and neck stretch.
2. **The peak.** For a brief moment the stretch is at its maximum. Many people also tense or stretch their arms and shoulders, and the eyes often squeeze shut or water.
3. **The quick exhale.** The breath goes out more rapidly than it came in, and the muscles relax.

Most scientists describe yawning as a largely **involuntary reflex** — you can't easily stop one that has started — although you can also yawn on purpose. Yawning starts remarkably early: fetuses begin yawning in the womb around 12 weeks after conception. Yawning tends to become less frequent as we get older.

## The main theories: why do we yawn?

There is no single agreed explanation, and it's possible that yawning does more than one job. Here is how the major ideas compare:

| Theory | The basic idea | How well it holds up |
|---|---|---|
| Staying alert | A yawn raises heart rate and stretches muscles, giving the brain a brief "wake-up" nudge | Fits well with yawning when tired or bored |
| Brain cooling | A yawn moves cooler air and blood toward the head, helping lower brain temperature | Supported by several studies, still being tested |
| Social signaling | Yawns communicate states like tiredness or boredom and help synchronize groups | Plausible, but likely a secondary role |
| More oxygen | Yawning brings in extra oxygen when levels are low | Largely abandoned — low oxygen doesn't increase yawning |
| Ear pressure | Yawning opens the tubes that balance ear pressure | Real effect, but swallowing does the same, so unlikely to be the main purpose |

### Staying alert

Think about when you yawn most: right after waking, before bed, and during dull, passive activities such as long drives or lectures. One idea is that the yawn acts as a small jolt to the system. During a yawn, heart rate rises and the big stretch of the face and jaw may send a burst of activity to the brain, helping you stay awake a little longer. This is part of why yawning and feeling sleepy go together so often — if you want to understand what the brain is doing when you finally do fall asleep, see [what happens to your body when you sleep](/articles/what-happens-to-your-body-when-you-sleep/).

### Cooling the brain

The brain works best within a narrow temperature range, and it produces a lot of heat. The **brain cooling hypothesis** suggests that yawning is triggered when brain temperature creeps up, and that the yawn helps bring it back down. The deep inhale draws in air that is usually cooler than the body, and the wide stretch of the jaw increases blood flow through the face and head.

Some of the evidence comes from animals. In rats, researchers recorded a rise in brain temperature just before a yawn, followed by a return to normal within a few minutes afterward. In a human study published in *Frontiers in Evolutionary Neuroscience*, heart rate rose during yawns — which would push more blood through the head — and the authors noted that yawning is most frequent after waking and just before sleep, times when brain temperature is shifting. In another small experiment, people holding a warm pack to their forehead yawned more while watching videos of yawns than people holding a cold pack.

Researchers are honest that this isn't settled. Most human studies measure indirect signals rather than brain temperature itself, so the cooling idea is promising but still being tested.

## Why is yawning contagious?

Contagious yawning is the strange part. Seeing someone yawn — or hearing a yawn, reading about one, or even thinking about it — can make you yawn too. Contagious yawns are different from the spontaneous ones you have when tired, and several patterns stand out:

- **Closeness matters.** People are more likely to catch a yawn from family members and close friends than from strangers.
- **It develops with age.** Young children don't usually catch yawns; susceptibility tends to appear around 4 to 5 years old.
- **It varies between people.** Some people catch yawns very easily and others rarely do. Contagious yawning appears to be less common in people who find social interaction more difficult, including many people on the autism spectrum.
- **Other animals do it too.** In a study from the Yerkes National Primate Research Center, chimpanzees watching short videos yawned about 50% more often after seeing members of their own group yawn than after seeing unfamiliar chimpanzees.

These patterns have led many researchers to link contagious yawning to **empathy** — our tendency to automatically mirror and share the states of people around us. Studies have found associations between contagious yawning and empathy scores. Still, the picture isn't completely clean. Dogs can catch yawns, but they don't seem to show the same "closer bond, more yawning" pattern, which has made some scientists cautious about treating yawning as a pure measure of empathy.

One practical view combines the theories: if yawning helps keep a brain alert, then a yawn spreading through a group might help everyone stay alert at the same time — useful for animals that live together and need to watch for danger.

## Everyday triggers, and can you stop a yawn?

The most common reason to yawn is simply being **tired**. Other everyday triggers include:

- Waking up or getting ready for sleep
- Boredom or long periods of sitting still
- Warm, stuffy rooms
- Stress or nervousness (athletes and performers often yawn before an event)
- Hunger
- Seeing, hearing or reading about someone else yawning

It's hard to stop a yawn once it begins, but some people find that breathing slowly through the nose, sipping a cool drink, or stepping into fresher, cooler air makes yawns less frequent — which fits with the brain cooling idea, though there's little formal research on these tricks. If you're yawning all day, the most useful step is usually addressing the cause, which is most often not enough sleep. It's also worth remembering that relying on stimulants to push through tiredness can backfire later; [how caffeine works in the brain](/articles/how-does-caffeine-work-in-the-brain/) explains why it masks sleepiness rather than removing it.

## When yawning could be a sign of something else

For almost everyone, yawning is completely normal. Adults yawn on average around nine times a day, and some healthy people yawn 20 times or more. Some clinicians consider yawning **excessive** when it happens more than about three times in 15 minutes, repeatedly, without an obvious reason such as tiredness.

Frequent yawning is most often linked to sleepiness — from too little sleep, poor-quality sleep or sleep disorders such as obstructive sleep apnea. Less commonly, it can be associated with certain medicines, problems with body temperature regulation, or conditions affecting the brain and nervous system.

**Talk to a healthcare professional** if you notice excessive yawning that has no clear explanation, lasts more than a few days, or comes with significant daytime sleepiness. **Seek emergency care right away** if unusual yawning appears together with signs of a stroke, such as sudden numbness or weakness in the face, arm or leg, or with chest pain.

## The takeaway

A yawn is a full-body reflex — a long stretch of the jaw and a deep breath that lasts just a few seconds. The best current explanations are that yawning gives the brain a small alertness boost and may help regulate its temperature, while the old "more oxygen" idea has fallen out of favor. Contagious yawning appears tied to social connection, which is why yawns spread fastest among people who are close. Ordinary yawning is harmless; a sudden, unexplained increase is worth mentioning to a doctor.
