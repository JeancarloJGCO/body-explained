You've probably heard that you should "eat more fiber," usually in the same breath as advice about bran cereal and regularity. But fiber does far more than keep things moving. It shapes how quickly you digest a meal, feeds trillions of microbes in your gut and is linked to better heart health. Oddly enough, your body can't actually digest it.

> **In short:** Dietary fiber is the part of plant foods your body can't break down. **Soluble fiber** forms a gel that slows digestion and can help lower LDL cholesterol, while **insoluble fiber** adds bulk that helps waste move through your intestines. Gut bacteria ferment some fiber into compounds that nourish the colon. U.S. guidance suggests about **14 grams per 1,000 calories** — roughly 25 grams a day for many women and 30 to 38 grams for many men.

## What is dietary fiber?

Fiber is a type of carbohydrate found in plants — fruits, vegetables, whole grains, beans, lentils, nuts and seeds. Most carbohydrates, such as starches and sugars, get broken down into glucose and absorbed into your blood. Fiber is different. Your digestive enzymes simply can't split it apart, so it travels through your stomach and small intestine mostly unchanged.

That might sound like fiber is just "filler." In reality, its journey through your gut is exactly what makes it useful. As it moves along, it absorbs water, changes the texture of what you've eaten, slows or speeds up transit, and eventually becomes food for the bacteria living in your large intestine.

Animal foods such as meat, eggs and dairy contain no fiber at all. Heavily processed foods like white bread and many snack foods usually have had much of their fiber removed during refining.

## Soluble vs. insoluble fiber: two different jobs

Fiber is traditionally sorted into two broad groups, based on whether it dissolves in water. Most plant foods contain both kinds, in different proportions.

| | Soluble fiber | Insoluble fiber |
|---|---|---|
| **What it does in water** | Dissolves and forms a soft, gel-like material | Doesn't dissolve; holds onto water and adds bulk |
| **Main effect** | Slows digestion and the absorption of sugar | Helps stool move through the intestines |
| **Linked benefits** | Can help lower LDL ("bad") cholesterol and steady blood sugar after meals | Supports regular bowel movements and softer, bulkier stool |
| **Good sources** | Oats, barley, beans, lentils, apples, citrus, carrots, psyllium | Whole-wheat products, wheat bran, nuts, many vegetables, the skins of fruits |

Think of soluble fiber as a thickener. When you stir oats into hot water and they turn creamy, you're seeing soluble fiber at work. In your gut, that gel slows the rate at which your stomach empties and the rate at which sugar reaches your bloodstream. It can also bind to bile acids, which are made from cholesterol. Your liver then pulls more cholesterol from the blood to make new bile — one reason soluble fiber is associated with lower LDL levels.

Insoluble fiber works more like a broom. It doesn't form a gel, but it holds water and gives stool more bulk and a softer texture. That makes it easier for the muscles in your intestinal wall to push things along.

## The journey of fiber through your gut, step by step

Following a bite of fiber-rich food through your digestive system shows how each stage benefits:

1. **Mouth.** Fibrous foods usually take longer to chew. That small delay gives your body more time to register that you're eating.
2. **Stomach.** Soluble fiber thickens the contents of your stomach, so the meal leaves more gradually. This is part of why high-fiber meals tend to feel more filling.
3. **Small intestine.** This is where most nutrients are absorbed. Because fiber slows things down, sugars from the meal enter the blood more gradually rather than in a sharp spike.
4. **Large intestine (colon).** Here, gut bacteria get to work. They ferment some types of fiber — mostly soluble kinds — and produce **short-chain fatty acids** such as butyrate, acetate and propionate. Butyrate is a major fuel source for the cells lining your colon.
5. **Exit.** Whatever isn't fermented, especially insoluble fiber, adds bulk to stool and leaves the body.

Fiber that feeds beneficial gut bacteria is sometimes called a *prebiotic*. Researchers are still mapping exactly how the gut microbiome influences health, but a diet rich in varied plant fibers is consistently linked with a more diverse community of gut microbes.

## What fiber is linked to in the long run

Beyond digestion, large population studies have connected higher-fiber diets with several health outcomes. These studies show associations, which means they can't prove that fiber alone is responsible — people who eat more fiber often have other healthy habits too. Still, the pattern is consistent:

- **Bowel health.** Fiber helps prevent constipation, and higher intake is associated with a lower risk of hemorrhoids and diverticular disease.
- **Heart health.** Diets rich in fiber, especially soluble fiber from foods like oats and beans, are associated with lower LDL cholesterol and a reduced risk of heart disease. (For more on the organ itself, see [how the heart pumps blood](/articles/how-does-the-heart-pump-blood/).)
- **Blood sugar.** By slowing sugar absorption, fiber helps prevent sharp rises in blood sugar after meals, and whole-grain, high-fiber diets are linked with a lower risk of type 2 diabetes.
- **Feeling full.** High-fiber foods are often bulkier and take longer to eat and digest, which can help you feel satisfied after a meal.

Whole foods also deliver fiber packaged with vitamins, minerals and other plant compounds, which is one reason health organizations generally recommend getting fiber from food first.

## How much fiber do you need?

U.S. dietary guidance is based on about **14 grams of fiber per 1,000 calories** you eat. For many adults, that lands around:

- **Women:** roughly 21 to 28 grams a day, depending on age
- **Men:** roughly 28 to 38 grams a day, depending on age

Needs tend to be a bit lower for older adults, who generally eat fewer calories. Most people in the U.S. and many other countries eat far less than these amounts — often only about half — so for most of us, the practical goal is simply "a little more than now."

Here's roughly how much fiber some everyday foods provide (amounts vary by brand and preparation):

| Food | Approximate fiber |
|---|---|
| Lentils, cooked (1 cup) | about 15 grams |
| Black beans, cooked (1 cup) | about 15 grams |
| Raspberries (1 cup) | about 8 grams |
| Whole-wheat spaghetti, cooked (1 cup) | about 6 grams |
| Pear, medium, with skin | about 5 to 6 grams |
| Broccoli, boiled (1 cup) | about 5 grams |
| Apple, medium, with skin | about 4 to 5 grams |
| Oatmeal, cooked (1 cup) | about 4 grams |
| Almonds (1 ounce, about 23 nuts) | about 3.5 grams |

## Easy ways to eat more fiber

You don't need special products to raise your fiber intake. A few swaps add up quickly:

- **Choose whole grains.** Pick whole-wheat bread, brown rice, oats, barley or whole-grain pasta instead of refined versions. Look for "whole" as the first word in the ingredient list.
- **Add beans or lentils.** Stir them into soups, salads, tacos or pasta sauce a few times a week. They're among the richest fiber sources there are.
- **Keep the skins on.** Much of the fiber in apples, pears and potatoes is in or just under the skin.
- **Eat fruit instead of drinking juice.** Juicing removes most of the fiber.
- **Snack on nuts, seeds or raw vegetables** rather than chips or crackers made from refined flour.
- **Aim for variety.** Different plants contain different types of fiber, and your gut bacteria seem to benefit from a mix.

Two tips make the change more comfortable. First, **go slowly**: adding a lot of fiber at once can cause gas, bloating and cramping while your gut bacteria adjust, so spread the increase over a few weeks. Second, **drink enough fluids**, because fiber works best when it can absorb water. If you're unsure how much that is, see [how much water you should drink a day](/articles/how-much-water-should-you-drink-a-day/).

## When to talk to a professional

For most healthy people, eating more fiber-rich foods is a safe, gradual change. Still, it's a good idea to check with a doctor or registered dietitian before making big changes if you have a digestive condition such as inflammatory bowel disease, have had bowel surgery, or have been advised to follow a low-fiber diet. You should also see a doctor about ongoing changes in bowel habits, persistent abdominal pain, or blood in your stool, rather than trying to fix them with diet alone.

## The takeaway

Fiber is the part of plant food your body can't digest — and that's exactly what makes it valuable. Soluble fiber forms a gel that slows digestion and helps manage cholesterol and blood sugar, while insoluble fiber adds bulk that keeps your bowels regular. Along the way, gut bacteria ferment some fiber into compounds that nourish your colon. Most people fall short of the roughly 14 grams per 1,000 calories recommended, so adding beans, whole grains, fruit and vegetables gradually, with plenty of fluids, is a simple step worth taking.
