You have probably heard that everyone needs eight hours of sleep. Then you meet someone who swears they feel great on six, and a teenager who could sleep until noon. So how much sleep do you actually need — and does the answer change as you get older?

> **In short:** Most adults need at least 7 hours of sleep a night. Children and teens need more: roughly 9 to 12 hours for school-age kids and 8 to 10 hours for teenagers, while babies and toddlers need 11 to 17 hours including naps. Needs fall steeply through childhood, then stay fairly stable through adulthood.

## Why sleep needs change with age

Sleep is not idle time. As our guide to [what happens to your body when you sleep](/articles/what-happens-to-your-body-when-you-sleep/) explains, the brain cycles through light sleep, deep sleep and REM sleep several times a night. Each stage does different work, from tissue repair and growth hormone release to sorting and storing memories.

That work is heaviest early in life. A newborn's brain is growing at a remarkable pace, so babies spend most of the day asleep, and a large share of that sleep is REM. As the brain matures, total sleep shrinks, deep sleep becomes more concentrated, and the body clock settles into a single long night.

A few other shifts are worth knowing:

- **Teenagers' body clocks run later.** During puberty, the internal clock tends to shift, so teens often don't feel sleepy until later at night. Early school starts then cut into the 8 to 10 hours they still need.
- **Adult needs are fairly steady.** From the early twenties to the sixties, the recommended amount barely changes.
- **Older adults sleep differently, not necessarily less.** Sleep tends to become lighter and more broken, and people often feel sleepy earlier in the evening and wake earlier in the morning.

## Recommended hours of sleep by age

The numbers below come from consensus statements by the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) for children and adults, and from the National Sleep Foundation for newborns and older adults. The CDC uses the same ranges in its public guidance. They are per 24 hours, and for young children they include naps.

| Age group | Recommended sleep per 24 hours |
|---|---|
| Newborn (0–3 months) | 14–17 hours |
| Infant (4–12 months) | 12–16 hours, including naps |
| Toddler (1–2 years) | 11–14 hours, including naps |
| Preschool (3–5 years) | 10–13 hours, including naps |
| School age (6–12 years) | 9–12 hours |
| Teen (13–18 years) | 8–10 hours |
| Adult (18–60 years) | 7 or more hours |
| Adult (61–64 years) | 7–9 hours |
| Adult (65+ years) | 7–8 hours |

These are ranges, not targets to hit to the minute. Some people naturally sit near the top of their range and some near the bottom. The AASM statement for adults also notes that sleeping more than 9 hours can be appropriate for young adults, for people paying back a sleep debt, and for people who are unwell.

## Why "at least 7 hours" for adults

The adult recommendation did not come from a single study. In 2015, a panel of sleep experts brought together by the AASM and the Sleep Research Society reviewed the research on sleep duration and health. They looked at links between sleep length and outcomes such as heart and metabolic health, immune function, mental health, mood, pain, performance and safety.

Their conclusion: adults should sleep **7 or more hours a night on a regular basis**. Consistently getting less than that was associated with a range of problems, including weight gain, high blood pressure, depression, weakened immune response, more errors and a higher risk of accidents.

Two honest caveats apply. First, much of this research shows associations, so it cannot always prove that short sleep causes a given problem. Second, people are poor judges of their own sleepiness. In lab studies where sleep is restricted night after night, performance keeps getting worse even after people say they have adjusted. Feeling "used to it" is not the same as being well rested.

## Signs you may not be getting enough

The table gives a starting point, but your body gives useful feedback too. You may be short on sleep if you notice:

1. You need an alarm every day and often hit snooze several times.
2. You fall asleep within a few minutes of lying down, almost every night.
3. You feel drowsy while reading, watching TV or sitting in meetings.
4. You rely on [caffeine](/articles/how-does-caffeine-work-in-the-brain/) to get through the afternoon.
5. You sleep much longer on days off than on workdays.
6. You feel more irritable, forgetful or less focused than usual.

A simple test: on a vacation with no alarm, after the first few nights of catch-up, note how long you naturally sleep. That number is a decent estimate of what your body needs.

## Can you catch up on lost sleep?

Sleeping in on the weekend can take the edge off a short week, and an occasional long night after a stretch of poor sleep is normal. But research suggests weekend catch-up only partly makes up for lost sleep. It does not seem to fully restore attention or reverse the metabolic effects of repeated short nights.

Big swings between weekday and weekend schedules can also push your body clock later, a bit like flying across a time zone every Friday. That can make it harder to fall asleep on Sunday night and leave you groggy on Monday.

What tends to help more:

- **Keep a regular wake time**, including on weekends, as much as you can.
- **Protect a sleep window** that gives you the hours your age group needs, plus a little time to fall asleep.
- **Get daylight in the morning** and keep evenings dimmer to help your body clock stay on schedule.
- **Watch late caffeine and alcohol**, both of which can disrupt sleep quality.
- **Keep naps short and early** — around 20 minutes in the early afternoon — if you nap at all.

## When to talk to a professional

Getting enough hours is only part of the picture; the quality of that sleep matters too. It is worth speaking with a doctor if you regularly sleep the recommended amount but still wake up exhausted, if someone notices you snoring loudly or pausing in your breathing, if you have trouble falling or staying asleep most nights for weeks, or if you feel dangerously sleepy while driving. For children, a pediatrician can help if a child seems persistently tired, struggles to wake, or has big changes in sleep or behavior.

## The takeaway

How much sleep you need depends mostly on your age. Babies and young children need the most, school-age kids and teens still need more than adults, and most adults need at least 7 hours a night, with similar needs into older age. Use the ranges as a guide, pay attention to how you feel during the day, and aim for a steady schedule rather than relying on weekend catch-up.
