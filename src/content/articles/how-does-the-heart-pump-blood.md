Put two fingers on the side of your neck and you'll feel it: a steady push, about once a second, that never takes a break. Your heart has been doing this since before you were born, and it will beat roughly 100,000 times today without asking for your attention. But what is actually happening inside that fist-sized muscle each time it squeezes?

> **In short:** The heart is a muscular pump with four chambers and four one-way valves. An electrical signal starts in a natural pacemaker, spreads across the heart, and makes the upper chambers squeeze blood into the lower chambers, which then squeeze it out — the right side to the lungs to pick up oxygen, the left side to the rest of the body.

## The heart is really two pumps side by side

It's easiest to think of the heart as two pumps joined together and working in sync. The **right side** collects blood that has already delivered its oxygen to the body and sends it on a short trip to the lungs. The **left side** receives freshly oxygenated blood from the lungs and sends it out to everything else, from your brain to your toes.

Each side has two chambers:

- **Atria (singular: atrium)** — the two upper chambers. They act like waiting rooms, receiving blood as it returns to the heart.
- **Ventricles** — the two lower chambers. These are the real pumps, with thick muscular walls that squeeze blood out of the heart.

A wall of muscle called the **septum** separates the left and right sides, so oxygen-poor and oxygen-rich blood don't mix. The left ventricle has the hardest job, because it has to generate enough pressure to push blood around the whole body. That's why its wall is noticeably thicker than the right ventricle's, which only needs to reach the nearby lungs.

## The path blood takes, step by step

Blood moves through the heart in a fixed loop. Four valves act like one-way doors, opening to let blood forward and closing to stop it from slipping backward. Here is the full route:

1. **Back from the body.** Oxygen-poor blood returns through two large veins, the superior and inferior vena cava, and fills the **right atrium**.
2. **Into the right ventricle.** The blood passes through the **tricuspid valve** into the right ventricle.
3. **Out to the lungs.** The right ventricle squeezes, pushing blood through the **pulmonary valve** into the pulmonary artery, which carries it to the lungs.
4. **Picking up oxygen.** In the lungs, blood releases carbon dioxide (which you breathe out) and picks up oxygen.
5. **Back to the heart.** Oxygen-rich blood returns through the pulmonary veins into the **left atrium**.
6. **Into the left ventricle.** It flows through the **mitral valve** into the left ventricle.
7. **Out to the body.** The left ventricle contracts strongly, sending blood through the **aortic valve** into the aorta, the body's largest artery, which branches into smaller arteries everywhere.

The trip from heart to lungs and back is called **pulmonary circulation**. The trip from heart to body and back is **systemic circulation**. Even though we describe the route as a sequence, both sides of the heart work at the same moment: the two atria fill and squeeze together, then the two ventricles do the same.

| Valve | Sits between | Job |
|---|---|---|
| Tricuspid | Right atrium and right ventricle | Lets blood into the right ventricle, then stops backflow |
| Pulmonary | Right ventricle and pulmonary artery | Lets blood out toward the lungs |
| Mitral | Left atrium and left ventricle | Lets blood into the left ventricle, then stops backflow |
| Aortic | Left ventricle and aorta | Lets blood out to the body |

The heart muscle itself needs oxygen too, and it doesn't absorb it from the blood passing through its chambers. Instead, the **coronary arteries** branch off the aorta right after it leaves the heart and wrap around the outside of the heart, feeding the muscle directly.

## What makes the heart beat: its built-in electrical system

Unlike the muscles in your arm, the heart doesn't wait for a command from the brain to contract. It has its own wiring, called the **cardiac conduction system**, and each heartbeat follows the same electrical path:

1. **The SA node fires.** The sinoatrial node, a small strip of tissue in the upper right atrium, is the heart's natural pacemaker. It sends out the electrical impulse that starts each beat.
2. **The atria contract.** The signal spreads across both atria, which squeeze and push blood down into the ventricles.
3. **The AV node pauses.** The signal reaches the atrioventricular node near the center of the heart, which holds it for a fraction of a second. That brief delay gives the atria time to finish emptying before the ventricles squeeze.
4. **The signal races down.** It travels along the bundle of His, a cord of fibers in the septum, which splits into left and right branches.
5. **The ventricles contract.** Fine Purkinje fibers spread the signal through the ventricle walls very quickly, so the ventricles squeeze together and push blood out to the lungs and body.

Your nervous system acts like a volume knob on this system. When you exercise or feel startled, signals from the "fight or flight" side of the nervous system speed the SA node up. When you rest, the "rest and digest" side slows it down — one reason your heart rate drops during deep sleep, as described in [what happens to your body when you sleep](/articles/what-happens-to-your-body-when-you-sleep/). If the SA node ever falters, other parts of the conduction system can step in as backup pacemakers, though usually at a slower rate.

## Squeeze and refill: the heartbeat in two phases

Every heartbeat has two main phases:

- **Systole** — the squeeze. The ventricles contract and push blood out of the heart.
- **Diastole** — the relaxation. The heart muscle relaxes and the chambers refill with blood, ready for the next beat.

The familiar **"lub-dub"** sound is the soundtrack of this cycle. Both sounds come from valves closing. The "lub" is the tricuspid and mitral valves shutting as the ventricles start to squeeze; the "dub" is the pulmonary and aortic valves shutting as the ventricles relax. When a doctor hears an extra whooshing sound between them, it's called a murmur. Many murmurs are harmless, but some are worth checking, which is one reason listening to the heart is part of a routine exam.

## How much work does the heart do?

The amount of blood your heart pumps each minute is called **cardiac output**. It's simply the amount pushed out with each beat (the *stroke volume*) multiplied by how many times the heart beats per minute.

| Measure | Typical figure |
|---|---|
| Resting heart rate (adults) | About 60–100 beats per minute |
| Cardiac output at rest | About 5–6 liters per minute |
| Cardiac output in a trained athlete during hard exercise | Can exceed 35 liters per minute |
| Heartbeats per day | Roughly 100,000 |
| Blood pumped per day | Roughly 2,000 gallons |
| Heartbeats over an 80-year life | Roughly 2.5 billion |

When you start moving, your muscles need more oxygen, so the heart responds by beating faster and pushing out more blood with each squeeze. That's the burst you feel during a run or a flight of stairs — and regular exercise trains the heart to handle that demand more easily, which is also why fit people tend to have a slower pulse at rest (see [what a good resting heart rate is](/articles/what-is-a-good-resting-heart-rate/)). (If your legs complain the next day, that's a different story, explained in [why your muscles are sore days after exercise](/articles/why-are-my-muscles-sore-after-exercise/).)

## When to talk to a professional

A healthy heart is remarkably reliable, but it's worth getting checked by a healthcare professional if you notice a heartbeat that often feels unusually fast, slow, fluttering or irregular, especially if it comes with dizziness or shortness of breath. Chest pain, pressure, or fainting are reasons to seek urgent medical care right away. Only a clinician can work out what is behind symptoms like these.

## The takeaway

Your heart is two coordinated pumps in one: the right side sends blood to the lungs to collect oxygen, and the left side sends that oxygen-rich blood to the rest of the body. Four one-way valves keep everything moving in the right direction, and their closing makes the "lub-dub" sound. Each beat starts with an electrical spark from the SA node, which is paused briefly at the AV node so the chambers squeeze in the right order. Repeated about 100,000 times a day, that simple rhythm keeps every cell in your body supplied.
