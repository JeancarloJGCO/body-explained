You lace your fingers together, push your palms outward, and hear a satisfying string of pops. Maybe someone nearby winces and tells you that you are going to give yourself arthritis. Knuckle cracking is one of the most common habits around, and for more than a century scientists have argued about what actually makes the sound. The good news is that we now have a fairly clear picture — and it is less alarming than the warnings suggest.

> **In short:** Knuckles crack because pulling or bending the joint quickly lowers the pressure in the fluid inside it, and a small gas-filled cavity suddenly forms. That rapid change makes the popping sound. Research so far has not found that cracking your knuckles causes arthritis.

## What is inside a knuckle joint?

Your knuckles are the joints where your finger bones meet each other and the bones of your hand. Like most of the movable joints in your body, each one is a **synovial joint**. That means:

- The ends of the two bones are covered in smooth, slippery **cartilage**.
- The joint is wrapped in a tough sleeve called the **joint capsule**.
- The capsule is filled with **synovial fluid**, a thick liquid that works like oil, letting the surfaces glide and feeding the cartilage.

Synovial fluid contains dissolved gases, such as carbon dioxide and nitrogen, much like a sealed bottle of soda contains dissolved gas you can't see. As long as the pressure inside the joint stays steady, those gases stay dissolved. The key to the crack is what happens when that pressure suddenly drops.

## What actually makes the popping sound

When you pull a finger, bend it backward or press your knuckles, you stretch the joint capsule and pull the joint surfaces slightly apart. The space inside gets a little bigger, but the amount of fluid stays the same, so the pressure inside the joint falls.

At a certain point, the surfaces separate quickly and a gas-filled cavity — essentially a bubble — forms in the fluid. This process is called **cavitation**. The sudden change is what you hear as a crack.

For decades, researchers disagreed about one detail: is the sound made by a bubble **forming**, or by a bubble **collapsing**? Two pieces of research helped settle the picture:

1. **Watching a knuckle crack in an MRI scanner.** In a 2015 study, researchers pulled on volunteers' fingers inside an MRI machine and filmed the joints in real time. They saw a dark cavity appear inside the joint at the exact moment of the crack. The cavity was still there after the sound, which suggested the pop is linked to the bubble forming, not disappearing.
2. **Building a mathematical model of the sound.** In 2018, two engineers created equations describing how pressure changes inside a knuckle. Their model suggested that the sound could come from the bubble **partly collapsing** right after it forms, and the predicted sound matched real recordings well.

These findings may seem to conflict, but they can fit together: a cavity forms quickly, part of it may shrink suddenly, and some gas remains behind for a while. Scientists are still refining the details, but they agree on the main point — the crack is about rapid pressure changes and gas in the joint fluid, not bones grinding or snapping.

## Why you can't crack the same knuckle twice in a row

If you crack a knuckle and immediately try again, it usually won't pop. Joints seem to have a **refractory period** — a reset time — often estimated at around 20 minutes.

The likely reason is that the gas left behind after a crack has to dissolve back into the fluid before the joint can build up the same pressure change again. Until then, the joint can stretch without that sudden snap. This is also why the joint may feel a bit looser or more mobile right after a crack.

## Not every joint noise is the same

Knuckle cracking is only one kind of joint sound. Other pops, clicks and creaks have different causes:

| Type of noise | Where you might notice it | Likely cause |
|---|---|---|
| Single loud crack that can't be repeated right away | Knuckles, spine, toes | Gas cavity forming in joint fluid (cavitation) |
| Snap or click with certain movements | Hip, ankle, shoulder | A tendon or ligament sliding over a bone |
| Grinding or crunching (crepitus) | Knees, shoulders | Rougher cartilage surfaces rubbing together |
| Creak or crackle when standing up | Knees | Often harmless, but can come with wear of the joint over time |

Most of these sounds are normal, especially when they don't hurt. Many people notice that their joints get noisier as they age, as cartilage becomes a little less smooth and tissues around the joint change. ## Does cracking your knuckles cause arthritis?

This is the question most people really want answered, and the evidence is reassuring.

- **The one-hand experiment.** A doctor named Donald Unger cracked the knuckles of his left hand, but not his right, at least twice a day for around 60 years. When he compared his hands later in life, neither showed arthritis, and there was no difference between them. It was only one person, but it became a famous illustration of the point.
- **Larger studies.** Studies comparing groups of habitual knuckle crackers with people who don't crack have not found higher rates of hand osteoarthritis among the crackers.
- **One mixed finding.** A 1990 study of 300 people found no link with arthritis, but did report that habitual crackers were more likely to have hand swelling and weaker grip strength. Later research has not consistently confirmed this, so the evidence on these minor effects is mixed.

In short, there is no good evidence that cracking your knuckles wears out your joints or leads to arthritis. That said, cracking can be a hard habit to break, and some people around you may find the sound annoying — which may be the biggest downside.

## What to know if you crack your joints

You don't need to stop cracking your knuckles for the sake of your joints, but a few common-sense points apply:

1. **Be gentle.** The habit itself appears harmless, but yanking or twisting a joint with force is never a good idea.
2. **Don't force a pop.** If a joint won't crack, it is probably in its reset period. Trying harder just adds strain.
3. **Treat it as a habit if it bothers you.** Many people crack their knuckles out of stress or boredom. Keeping your hands busy, stretching your fingers gently or noticing when you tend to do it can help if you want to cut back.
4. **Keep joints moving.** Regular movement keeps joint fluid circulating and the tissues around joints healthy. Something as simple as [walking every day](/articles/what-happens-when-you-walk-every-day/) supports your joints and muscles.

## When to see a professional

A painless pop is almost always nothing to worry about. It is worth getting checked by a doctor if joint noises come with:

- Pain, especially pain that is new or getting worse
- Swelling, warmth or redness around the joint
- A joint that locks, catches or gives way
- Reduced movement, or difficulty straightening or bending the joint
- A pop that happened during an injury, such as a fall or a sports collision

These signs suggest something other than harmless gas bubbles may be going on, and a professional can examine the joint and explain what is happening.

## The takeaway

The crack of a knuckle is the sound of physics inside your joint: stretching the joint lowers the pressure in its fluid, and a gas cavity suddenly forms. Scientists have watched this happen on MRI and modeled the sound with math, and the details are still being refined. Despite the old warnings, studies so far have not linked knuckle cracking to arthritis. Painless pops are normal, but joint noises that come with pain, swelling or locking deserve a closer look.
