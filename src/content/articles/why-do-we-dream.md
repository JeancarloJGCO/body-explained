You're running through an airport you've never seen, your old math teacher is the pilot, and somehow you're late for an exam. Then the alarm goes off, and within a minute the whole scene has dissolved. Nearly everyone has nights like this. So why does the brain bother putting on a show while we sleep — and why do we forget most of it?

> **In short:** Nobody knows for certain why we dream. Dreams are produced by the brain's normal activity during sleep, especially REM sleep, and the leading theories suggest they help us process emotions, strengthen memories and rehearse for real-life challenges. Most dreams are forgotten because the brain's memory-saving systems are relatively switched off while we dream.

## What happens in your brain when you dream

Dreaming is tied to the rhythm of the sleep cycle. As explained in our guide to [what happens to your body when you sleep](/articles/what-happens-to-your-body-when-you-sleep/), you cycle through light sleep, deep sleep and REM (rapid eye movement) sleep roughly every 90 minutes. Your first REM period usually starts about an hour and a half after you fall asleep, and REM periods grow longer toward morning.

According to the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke (NINDS), people spend about **two hours a night dreaming**, though most of it is never remembered. During REM sleep:

- **Brain waves look a lot like wakefulness.** The brain is busy, not resting.
- **The thalamus relays images and sounds** to the outer layer of the brain, the cortex, which helps build the dream "scene."
- **Arm and leg muscles are temporarily paralyzed.** Signals from the brainstem keep you from physically acting out what you're dreaming.
- **Breathing, heart rate and blood pressure rise** toward daytime levels.

Brain-imaging studies reviewed by sleep researchers Yuval Nir and Giulio Tononi show a distinctive pattern. Areas involved in emotion and memory (such as the amygdala and hippocampus) and areas that handle visual imagery become more active. Meanwhile, parts of the prefrontal cortex — the region we rely on for logic, planning and noticing that something is "off" — are less active. That mix helps explain why dreams feel vivid and emotional but rarely question their own strange logic.

Brain chemistry shifts too. Cleveland Clinic notes that chemical messengers such as acetylcholine and dopamine are relatively high during REM sleep, while serotonin, norepinephrine and histamine are dialed down. Researchers think this unusual chemical balance contributes to the bizarre, hard-to-hold-onto quality of dreams.

### Not only a REM thing

For decades, REM sleep and dreaming were treated as basically the same thing. Newer research paints a messier picture. People woken from non-REM sleep often report dreams as well, and a meaningful share of those reports can't be distinguished from REM dreams. Some studies of people with damage to specific brain areas found they stopped dreaming even though their REM sleep continued. So REM sleep seems to make dreaming more likely and more vivid, but the two are not identical.

## The main theories about why we dream

Scientists have proposed many explanations, and they aren't mutually exclusive. Dreams may serve several purposes at once — or some of their features may simply be side effects of a sleeping brain. Here are the most discussed ideas:

| Theory | The basic idea | How well supported? |
|---|---|---|
| Emotional processing | Dreams help the brain work through emotional experiences and take some of the sting out of them | Growing evidence, especially from REM sleep research |
| Memory consolidation | Dreams reflect the brain replaying and sorting recent experiences while it stores them | Sleep clearly helps memory; dreams' exact role is unclear |
| Threat or rehearsal simulation | Dreams let us "practice" responding to challenges in a safe setting | Plausible and widely discussed; still debated |
| By-product (neurocognitive) view | Dreams are what a sleeping brain produces when it draws on stored memories and thoughts, without a special function of their own | A serious scientific position; hard to prove or disprove |
| Wish fulfillment (Freud) | Dreams express hidden desires in disguised form | Historically influential but not backed by modern evidence |

### Dreams as an emotional workshop

One of the best-supported ideas is that dreaming helps us handle emotions. A 2019 review in *Frontiers in Psychology* found that dreams reported after REM sleep tend to be especially emotional and vivid, and that REM sleep appears to play a central role in processing emotionally important experiences from waking life. Brain scans suggest dreaming uses some of the same networks that regulate emotion when we're awake.

The authors suggest dreams may work a bit like a simulation, where upsetting material can be revisited and gradually defused. They also propose that the strange, jumbled details of dreams might help soften the emotional charge of difficult memories. NINDS likewise notes that dreaming may help process emotions, though its exact purpose isn't known.

### Dreams and memory

Sleep is clearly important for learning — both non-REM and REM sleep seem to help lock in new memories (here's [how memories form in the brain](/articles/how-do-memories-form-in-the-brain/) in the first place). Dreams often borrow fragments of recent days: a conversation, a place, a worry. Cleveland Clinic describes dreaming as a likely helper in analyzing and consolidating memories, including skills and habits. Still, it's hard to prove whether the dream itself does the work or is simply a window into memory processing happening in the background.

### Rehearsal for real life

Another family of theories says dreams let us rehearse. Being chased, falling, showing up unprepared — common dream themes often involve threats or social stress. The idea is that "practicing" these scenarios in a safe, offline setting could have helped our ancestors respond better when they happened for real. It's an appealing idea, but researchers still disagree about how strong the evidence is.

### Maybe dreams don't need a purpose

Some scientists argue that dreams are a side effect rather than a tool. In this view, the sleeping brain keeps generating thoughts and images from stored memories, and we experience that as dreaming. Nir and Tononi suggest dreaming may be closer to imagination than to perception — built from the "inside out" rather than from incoming sensory information. That would fit with sudden scene changes, people whose identity keeps shifting and how hard dreams are to remember.

## Why we forget most of our dreams

If you dream for around two hours a night, why do you wake up remembering so little? Several factors seem to combine:

1. **The memory-saving system is quiet.** As Scientific American explains, the brain regions that shift experiences into long-term storage are relatively inactive during REM sleep.
2. **Short-term memory is brief.** Short-term memory is active while you dream, but it holds information for only about half a minute.
3. **You have to wake up at the right moment.** You generally remember a dream only if you wake during or right after it. If you slide into the next sleep stage instead, the dream is usually gone.
4. **Morning dreams have the best chance.** REM periods get longer as the night goes on, so the last dream before waking is the one most often remembered. Cutting sleep short also means less REM time overall.
5. **People differ.** Traits such as imaginativeness, openness to experience and how much attention you pay to your inner life have been linked to better dream recall.

## How to remember your dreams better

If you'd like to recall more of your dreams, a few simple habits can help:

- **Stay still for a moment when you wake.** Try to replay the dream before you move, check your phone or start thinking about the day.
- **Write it down right away.** Keep a notebook by the bed. Even a few keywords can bring back more details later.
- **Get enough sleep.** Since the longest REM periods come toward morning, a full night gives you more dream time to remember.
- **Set the intention.** Simply deciding before bed that you want to remember your dreams seems to help some people.

Keep in mind that things that disrupt sleep — including a late-afternoon coffee (see [how caffeine works in the brain](/articles/how-does-caffeine-work-in-the-brain/)) — can affect the overall structure of your night, including REM sleep.

## What dreams can and can't tell you

Dream dictionaries that claim a snake or a lost tooth has a fixed meaning are not supported by science. Cleveland Clinic points out that Freud's dream interpretations have never been substantiated and that interpreting dreams is highly personal. That said, dreams often echo what's on your mind — stress at work, a relationship, an upcoming change. Looking at your waking life is usually more useful than looking up symbols.

Occasional bad dreams are a normal part of being human. However, frequent nightmares that disturb your sleep, leave you dreading bedtime or follow a traumatic experience are worth mentioning to a doctor or sleep specialist, because effective support exists. The same goes for physically acting out dreams — such as shouting, punching or kicking in your sleep — since that normal REM muscle paralysis is supposed to prevent it.

## The takeaway

We all dream, most likely for a couple of hours every night, and most of it happens during the vivid, brain-busy stage of REM sleep. Science hasn't settled why we dream, but the strongest ideas point to emotional processing, memory and mental rehearsal — or to dreams being a natural by-product of a sleeping brain. We forget most dreams because the brain's memory-saving systems are quiet during REM. If you want to remember more, stay still when you wake and jot down what you can before it fades.
