Protein has a reputation as the "gym nutrient" — the thing in shakes and bars that bodybuilders talk about. But protein matters just as much to someone who never lifts a weight. It's in every cell you have, from the muscle in your heart to the enzymes that digest your lunch. The word itself comes from the Greek *protos*, meaning "first," which is a fair summary of how important it is.

> **In short:** Protein is a nutrient made of long chains of smaller units called **amino acids**. Your body breaks the protein you eat into amino acids, then reassembles them into thousands of proteins it needs to build and repair tissue, fight infection and run chemical reactions. Nine amino acids are **essential**, meaning they must come from food. Common U.S. guidance sets a baseline of about **0.8 grams per kilogram of body weight** a day for healthy adults, and most people already meet it.

## What is protein, exactly?

At the chemical level, a protein is a chain of amino acids folded into a specific three-dimensional shape. Think of amino acids as letters and proteins as words: with about 20 different letters, your body can spell out an enormous variety of words, each with its own job.

When you eat a piece of chicken, a bowl of lentils or a cup of yogurt, your digestive system doesn't absorb that protein as-is. Stomach acid and enzymes unfold and cut the chains, and by the time the pieces reach your small intestine they've been broken down into individual amino acids and very short chains. These are absorbed into your blood and sent to cells all over the body, which use them to build whatever proteins they need at the moment. (If you're curious how the rest of a meal travels through your gut, our guide to [what fiber does in your body](/articles/what-does-fiber-do-in-your-body/) follows a part of food your body *can't* break down.)

Protein is one of three macronutrients that provide energy, along with carbohydrate and fat. Each gram of protein supplies about 4 calories. But unlike the other two, protein's main value isn't fuel — it's raw material.

## What does protein do in your body?

Muscle is the obvious answer, but it's only the start. Protein is involved in almost everything your body does:

| Role | What it means in practice |
|---|---|
| **Structure** | Collagen and keratin give strength to skin, bone, tendons, hair and nails. |
| **Movement** | Muscle fibers contract using proteins called actin and myosin. |
| **Chemical reactions** | Enzymes — which are proteins — speed up digestion, energy production and countless other processes. |
| **Defense** | Antibodies that recognize and help neutralize germs are proteins. |
| **Transport** | Hemoglobin carries oxygen in red blood cells; other proteins move fats, vitamins and minerals through the blood. |
| **Signaling** | Some hormones, such as insulin, are proteins, and certain amino acids are used to make brain messengers like serotonin. |
| **Repair and growth** | New cells constantly replace old ones, and children, teens and pregnant people need extra protein for growth. |

Your body is also always recycling. Old proteins are broken down and their amino acids reused, while some are lost each day. Because the body doesn't keep a dedicated protein reserve the way it stores fat, it needs a regular supply from food to keep up.

## Essential, nonessential and conditional amino acids

Not all amino acids have to come from your plate. Scientists sort them into three groups:

1. **Essential amino acids (9).** Your body can't make these, so you must get them from food: histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan and valine.
2. **Nonessential amino acids.** Your body can make these on its own from other molecules, so it doesn't matter as much whether they're in your diet.
3. **Conditionally essential amino acids.** These, such as arginine, glutamine and tyrosine, are normally made in adequate amounts but may be needed from food during illness or periods of stress.

"Nonessential" is a slightly misleading name. These amino acids are every bit as important for building proteins — the label only means you don't have to eat them directly.

## Complete vs. incomplete proteins

Foods differ in how much of each essential amino acid they contain, and that's where the terms "complete" and "incomplete" come from.

- **Complete proteins** contain all nine essential amino acids in good proportions. Most animal foods fall into this group — meat, poultry, fish, eggs and dairy — along with whole soy foods such as tofu, tempeh and edamame.
- **Incomplete proteins** contain all nine but are low in one or more of them. Most plant foods, including beans, lentils, nuts, seeds, whole grains and vegetables, are in this group.

For a long time, people eating mostly plant foods were told to "combine" proteins at every meal — rice with beans, say, or peanut butter on whole-grain bread. Current guidance is more relaxed. Your body pools amino acids from everything you eat over the day, so eating a **variety** of plant proteins across your meals covers your needs. What matters is the overall pattern, not each individual plate.

## How much protein do you need?

In the United States, the Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) for healthy adults is about **0.8 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight** per day — roughly 0.36 grams per pound. Another common way to express it is that protein can make up anywhere from **10% to 35%** of total daily calories.

It helps to know what the RDA is and isn't. It's set as the amount that meets the basic needs of nearly all healthy adults and prevents deficiency. It's a floor, not a target you have to hit precisely, and research on whether more is better for most people is still evolving.

Needs can be higher in some situations:

- **Growth** in children and teens, and during pregnancy and breastfeeding.
- **Older age.** Muscle mass naturally starts to decline from around middle age, and some experts suggest older adults may benefit from somewhat more protein, alongside strength-building activity, to help preserve muscle.
- **Regular hard training,** which increases the need for repair. (Our article on [why muscles get sore after exercise](/articles/why-are-my-muscles-sore-after-exercise/) explains the micro-damage that protein helps fix.)
- **Recovery from illness or injury.**

Many nutrition experts also suggest spreading protein across meals rather than packing most of it into dinner, since the body seems to use moderate amounts at a time efficiently. The evidence on exact per-meal amounts is still debated, so treat this as a sensible habit rather than a strict rule.

## Where to find protein in food

You don't need special products to get enough protein. Here are typical amounts in everyday foods:

| Food | Approximate protein |
|---|---|
| 3 oz cooked fish, such as salmon or tuna | 21 g |
| 3 oz cooked chicken or turkey | 19 g |
| 6 oz plain Greek yogurt | 17 g |
| 1 cup cooked lentils | 17 g |
| 1/2 cup cottage cheese | 14 g |
| 1 cup milk | 8 g |
| 1/2 cup cooked beans | 8 g |
| 1 cup cooked quinoa | 8 g |
| 1 oz nuts | 6–7 g |
| 1 large egg | 6 g |

The "package" a protein comes in also matters. Beans, lentils, nuts and whole grains bring fiber, vitamins and minerals along with protein. Fish brings healthy fats. Heavily processed and red meats, on the other hand, are linked with health risks when eaten often, so nutrition experts generally advise against leaning on them to boost protein. Whole foods are usually a better choice than protein supplements for most people.

## Can you eat too much protein?

For healthy people, ordinary diets rarely cause problems, but more isn't automatically better. Because your body can't store extra protein for later, what it doesn't need gets burned for energy or stored as fat. Very high-protein diets built around fatty meats can also crowd out fiber-rich plant foods.

Processing protein creates waste products that your kidneys filter out — a job explained in our guide to [how your kidneys filter blood](/articles/how-do-kidneys-filter-blood/). For people with healthy kidneys, normal protein intakes are handled easily. If you have a kidney condition or another long-term health issue, protein needs can be quite different, and that's a conversation to have with your doctor or a registered dietitian rather than something to adjust on your own.

## The takeaway

Protein is a chain of amino acids that your body takes apart and rebuilds into everything from muscle and skin to enzymes and antibodies. Nine of those amino acids are essential and must come from food, but a varied diet — animal foods, plant foods or both — supplies them easily. Most healthy adults already get at least the recommended baseline of about 0.8 grams per kilogram a day. Focusing on a mix of good-quality protein foods spread across your meals is a simple, evidence-based way to cover your needs.
