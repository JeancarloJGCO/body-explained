Every few hours you head to the bathroom without giving it a second thought. But that simple routine is the last step of one of the busiest jobs in your body. Two organs, each about the size of your fist, quietly clean your entire blood supply many times a day. So how do your kidneys filter blood, and how do they decide what to keep and what to throw away?

> **In short:** Your kidneys contain about a million tiny filtering units called nephrons. In each one, a ball of blood vessels called the glomerulus pushes water and small molecules out of the blood, and a long tubule then pulls back nearly everything the body still needs. What's left over — extra water, salts and waste — becomes urine.

## Where your kidneys are and what they're up against

You have two bean-shaped kidneys tucked against the back wall of your abdomen, one on each side of the spine, just below the rib cage. Each is roughly 4 to 5 inches long.

For their size, they handle a huge amount of traffic. At rest, a little over 5 cups of blood flow into the kidneys every minute through the renal arteries, which branch directly off the body's main artery. That's a large share of everything the heart pumps (if you're curious how that pumping works, see our guide on [how the heart pumps blood](/articles/how-does-the-heart-pump-blood/)).

Why so much? Because nearly every cell in your body produces waste as it works. Muscles make creatinine, breaking down protein produces urea, and the foods and drinks you take in can push salt, potassium and acid levels up or down. The kidneys are the main place where all of this gets sorted out.

## Inside a nephron: the two-step filter

The real work happens in the nephrons. Each kidney has about a million of them, and every one is a tiny, self-contained filtering machine with two main parts.

**Step 1: The glomerulus does a rough first pass.** The glomerulus is a tight knot of very small blood vessels wrapped in a cup-shaped capsule. Blood arrives under pressure, and that pressure forces fluid through the vessel walls into the capsule. The filter works mostly by size: water, salts, glucose, amino acids and small wastes slip through, while blood cells and most proteins are too big and stay in the blood.

The fluid that collects in the capsule is called filtrate. At this stage it's not urine yet — it still holds plenty of things your body can't afford to lose.

**Step 2: The tubule takes back what's useful.** The filtrate then flows down a long, winding tube. Cells lining the tubule act like careful sorters. They reabsorb almost all of the water, nearly all of the glucose and amino acids, and most of the sodium, potassium and other minerals, returning them to tiny blood vessels that wrap around the tubule.

Physiology textbooks describe a third process too: **secretion**. Here the tubule actively moves certain substances from the blood *into* the filtrate, such as extra acid or potassium. Together, filtration, reabsorption and secretion decide the final makeup of your urine.

## The numbers behind the filtering

The scale of this process is surprising. Here's a quick look at what goes in and what comes out.

| What happens | Approximate amount |
|---|---|
| Blood entering the kidneys' filters | About half a cup per minute |
| Fluid filtered per day | Roughly 150–200 quarts, depending on the source |
| Water reabsorbed by the tubules | About 99% of what is filtered |
| Urine produced per day (adults) | About 1–2 quarts |
| Filtering units per kidney | About 1 million nephrons |

In other words, your kidneys filter far more fluid than you could ever drink, then hand almost all of it back. One anatomy textbook estimates that your total blood volume passes through the filters around 300 times a day. Children make less urine than adults, and the exact amount for anyone shifts with how much they drink, how much they sweat and what they eat.

## How the kidneys fine-tune your urine

The kidneys don't run on a fixed setting. Hormones adjust them from minute to minute so your body's internal balance stays steady.

1. **When you're low on fluid,** your brain releases antidiuretic hormone (ADH). It signals the final stretch of tubules, the collecting ducts, to pull more water back into the blood. Your urine becomes darker and more concentrated.
2. **When you've had plenty to drink,** less ADH is released, more water stays in the tubules, and urine turns paler and more plentiful. (Our article on [how much water you should drink a day](/articles/how-much-water-should-you-drink-a-day/) covers why urine color is a rough hydration clue.)
3. **When the body needs to hold onto salt,** a hormone called aldosterone tells the tubules to reabsorb more sodium, and water tends to follow.
4. **When blood acid creeps up,** the tubules can secrete more acid into the urine and hold onto buffering compounds.

This constant adjustment is why the kidneys are often described as the body's chemists rather than just its filters.

## More than a filter: the kidneys' other jobs

Making urine is the best-known task, but the kidneys also act as hormone factories.

- **Blood pressure:** When blood flow to the kidneys drops, they release an enzyme called renin. It sets off a chain of signals that tightens blood vessels and helps the body hold onto salt and water, which raises blood pressure.
- **Red blood cells:** The kidneys make erythropoietin, a hormone that tells the bone marrow to produce red blood cells. Since red blood cells carry oxygen, this links kidney health to energy levels.
- **Bones:** The kidneys convert vitamin D into its active form, calcitriol, which helps the gut absorb calcium for strong bones.
- **Blood sugar backup:** The kidneys can even make a small amount of glucose when blood sugar runs low.

## How kidney function is measured

Doctors often describe how well the kidneys are working with a number called the **glomerular filtration rate (GFR)** — roughly, how much blood the glomeruli clean each minute. Measuring it directly takes several hours of testing, so it's usually *estimated* (eGFR) from a simple blood test for creatinine, combined with factors such as age and body size. A related protein called cystatin C is sometimes used too.

Results vary with age, muscle mass and other health conditions, and kidney filtering tends to slow gradually as people get older, even without disease. Because of all these factors, a single number is best interpreted by a clinician who knows your health history.

## When to talk to a professional

Kidney problems often cause no obvious symptoms in their early stages, which is why routine check-ups and blood or urine tests can matter. It's reasonable to talk with a healthcare professional if you notice changes such as blood in your urine, foamy urine, swelling in your legs or around your eyes, or a big change in how often you urinate. People with high blood pressure, diabetes or a family history of kidney disease may be advised to get their kidney function checked regularly.

## The takeaway

Your kidneys clean your blood with about two million microscopic filters working in parallel. Each nephron first strains water and small molecules out of the blood, then carefully reclaims nearly everything the body still needs, leaving behind a small amount of concentrated waste as urine. Along the way, the kidneys help steer blood pressure, red blood cell production and bone health. It's a quiet, constant job — and one worth appreciating the next time you head to the bathroom.
