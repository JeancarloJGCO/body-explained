"Drink eight glasses of water a day" might be the most repeated health advice on the planet. It's simple and easy to remember — but it isn't where the science actually lands. The real answer is a little more flexible, and for most people, a lot less stressful.

> **In short:** The U.S. National Academies set an *adequate intake* of about **3.7 liters (15.5 cups) of total water per day for men** and **2.7 liters (11.5 cups) for women**. That includes water from **all drinks and food** — and food typically supplies around 20% of it. Needs go up with heat, exercise, pregnancy and breastfeeding.

## Why your body needs water

Water makes up roughly half to two-thirds of your body weight, and nearly every system depends on it. Water helps you:

- **Regulate body temperature** through sweating.
- **Move nutrients** and oxygen through the bloodstream.
- **Remove waste** through urine and bowel movements.
- **Cushion joints** and protect sensitive tissues like the spinal cord.

You lose water constantly — through breathing, sweating, urination and digestion — so it has to be replaced every day.

## What the official recommendation actually says

The most widely cited guidance comes from a 2004 report by the U.S. National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (then the Institute of Medicine). Rather than a strict requirement, the panel set **adequate intake levels** based on how much healthy, well-hydrated people actually consume:

| Group | Total water per day | Roughly from drinks | Roughly from food |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adult men (19+) | ~3.7 L (125 oz / 15.5 cups) | ~3.0 L (13 cups) | ~0.7 L |
| Adult women (19+) | ~2.7 L (91 oz / 11.5 cups) | ~2.2 L (9 cups) | ~0.5 L |

Two details matter here:

1. **These numbers are total water, not just plain water.** Coffee, tea, milk, juice, soup and water-rich foods all count.
2. **They describe healthy, mostly sedentary adults in temperate climates.** People who are very active or live in hot climates may need more.

## Where did "8 glasses a day" come from?

Eight 8-ounce glasses equals about **1.9 liters**. On its own, that's below the adequate intake for total water. But once you add the water you get from food and other beverages, a person drinking roughly 2 liters of fluids a day often ends up close to the recommendation. In other words, "8 glasses" isn't wildly wrong — it's just an oversimplification. It also ignores the big differences between a desk worker in a cool office and a construction worker in summer.

## Food counts more than you think

Many foods are mostly water:

- **Cucumber, lettuce, celery:** about 95% water
- **Watermelon, strawberries:** about 90%
- **Oranges, peaches:** about 85–88%
- **Yogurt, cooked oatmeal, soups:** high water content too

A diet rich in fruits and vegetables quietly supplies a meaningful share of your daily water.

## What about coffee and tea?

Caffeine has a mild diuretic effect, which led to the old idea that coffee "dehydrates" you. For people who drink it regularly, that effect is small, and caffeinated drinks still contribute to your daily fluid intake. Plain water remains the best everyday choice because it has no calories or added sugar, but your morning coffee isn't working against you.

## Factors that raise your needs

You'll need more fluid when:

- **You exercise** or do physical work — especially for longer than an hour.
- **It's hot or humid**, or you're at high altitude.
- **You're sick** with fever, vomiting or diarrhea.
- **You're pregnant or breastfeeding.**

## Simple ways to tell if you're drinking enough

You don't need an app or a measuring jug. For most healthy adults, two everyday signals work well:

- **You rarely feel thirsty.** Thirst is a reliable cue in healthy adults (though it can become less sensitive with age).
- **Your urine is pale yellow or nearly clear.** Dark yellow urine usually means you could use more fluid.

Common signs of mild dehydration include thirst, dark urine, dry mouth, headache, fatigue and dizziness.

## Hydration changes with age

Fluid needs and thirst signals aren't constant across a lifetime:

- **Children** have higher water needs relative to their body size and may get absorbed in play without noticing thirst, so offering water regularly — especially in hot weather or during sports — is helpful.
- **Older adults** often experience a **weaker thirst response**, and the [kidneys](/articles/how-do-kidneys-filter-blood/) become less efficient at conserving water. Some medications, such as diuretics, also increase fluid loss. This makes older adults more prone to dehydration even when they don't feel thirsty, so building drinks into daily routines (with meals, with medications) can be useful.
- **Athletes and outdoor workers** can lose a liter or more of sweat per hour in hot conditions, along with salts like sodium. During long or intense sessions, replacing both fluid and electrolytes matters.

## Can you drink too much?

It's uncommon, but yes. Drinking very large amounts of water in a short time can overwhelm the kidneys' ability to excrete it, diluting the sodium in your blood. This condition, **hyponatremia**, can be serious and has occurred in endurance athletes and in water-drinking contests. For everyday life, simply drinking to thirst avoids both extremes.

## Practical tips

- Keep a reusable water bottle where you can see it.
- Drink a glass of water with each meal.
- Eat plenty of fruits and vegetables (which also supply water-loving [dietary fiber](/articles/what-does-fiber-do-in-your-body/)).
- Drink more before, during and after exercise or time in the heat.
- Choose water over sugary drinks most of the time.

People with certain health conditions — such as heart failure or kidney disease — may be given specific fluid limits by their doctor, and those instructions take priority over general guidance.

## The takeaway

There's no single magic number. A reasonable benchmark for healthy adults is about 2.7 liters of total water a day for women and 3.7 liters for men, counting everything you drink and eat. In practice, drinking when you're thirsty, eating water-rich foods and checking that your urine stays pale is enough for most people.
