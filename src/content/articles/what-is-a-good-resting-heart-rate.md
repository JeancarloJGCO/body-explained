Smartwatches have turned resting heart rate into a number many of us glance at every morning. But what does it actually mean if yours is 58, 72 or 85? Is lower always better, and why does it seem to jump around from day to day? Here's what your resting heart rate tells you about your body, and what it doesn't.

> **In short:** For most adults, a normal resting heart rate is **60 to 100 beats per minute**. Very fit people often sit lower, around **40 to 60**. Fitness, age, sleep, stress, caffeine, illness and some medicines all shift it, so the most useful thing to watch is your own trend over weeks, not a single reading.

## What resting heart rate actually measures

Your resting heart rate is how many times your heart beats in one minute when you are awake but calm and still, for example sitting or lying down after a few quiet minutes. At that moment, your heart is only supplying what your body needs to keep the basics running: your brain, organs and muscles at idle.

Each beat starts with an electrical signal from a small cluster of cells in the heart called the sinoatrial node, the heart's natural pacemaker. (For the full picture of how that pump works, see [how the heart pumps blood](/articles/how-does-the-heart-pump-blood/).) Your nervous system then fine-tunes that rhythm all day long. The "rest and digest" side slows it down, and the "fight or flight" side speeds it up.

How much blood your body needs per minute stays fairly steady at rest. The heart can deliver it in two ways: many smaller beats, or fewer, stronger beats. That trade-off is the key to understanding why resting heart rate varies so much between people.

## Normal resting heart rate by age

Children's hearts beat faster than adults' hearts, and the rate gradually slows through childhood. The ranges below come from the U.S. National Library of Medicine's MedlinePlus encyclopedia. Other medical sources use slightly different cutoffs, especially for babies, so treat these as general guides.

| Age group | Typical resting heart rate (beats per minute) |
|---|---|
| Newborns (0–1 month) | 70–190 |
| Infants (1–11 months) | 80–160 |
| Children 1–2 years | 80–130 |
| Children 3–4 years | 80–120 |
| Children 5–6 years | 75–115 |
| Children 7–9 years | 70–110 |
| Children 10+ and adults, including older adults | 60–100 |
| Well-trained athletes | 40–60 |

In medical terms, an adult resting rate under 60 is called bradycardia (slow heart rate) and one over 100 is called tachycardia (fast heart rate). Those labels describe the number, not whether something is wrong. A fit runner with a pulse of 48 can be perfectly healthy.

## So what counts as a "good" resting heart rate?

There isn't one perfect number. Still, research gives some useful context.

A 2016 meta-analysis in the *Canadian Medical Association Journal* pooled 46 long-term studies that followed more than a million adults. People with higher resting heart rates had a higher risk of dying during the follow-up periods. On average, every 10 extra beats per minute was linked to about a **9% higher risk of death from any cause** and an **8% higher risk of death from heart and blood vessel disease**. Compared with the lowest group, people with resting rates above 80 had noticeably higher risk.

It's important to read this carefully:

- **These are associations, not proof of cause.** A higher resting rate can be a sign of lower fitness, poor sleep, stress or an underlying condition, and those factors may drive much of the risk.
- **The researchers found signs of bias and big differences between studies,** so the exact percentages are less certain than they look.
- **It doesn't make the 90s "dangerous" overnight.** It simply suggests that, across large groups, a lower resting rate within the normal range tends to go along with better long-term health.

A practical way to think about it: somewhere in the 50s to 70s is common for healthy, active adults, and a gradual drop over months as you get fitter is usually a good sign.

## Why exercise lowers your resting heart rate

Regular aerobic exercise, such as brisk walking, cycling, swimming or running, is one of the most reliable ways resting heart rate falls over time. Here's why:

1. **The heart muscle gets stronger.** With training, the heart can push out more blood with each beat (a larger "stroke volume").
2. **Fewer beats do the same job.** Since each beat moves more blood, your heart doesn't need to beat as often at rest.
3. **The built-in pacemaker resets.** Scientists long assumed this was mostly the calming nervous system working harder. But a 2016 study that trained 51 healthy younger and older adults for six months found something else. Their average resting rate fell from about 63 to 58 beats per minute, yet their nervous system responses looked unchanged. The authors concluded that the slowdown was most likely due to a lower "intrinsic" rate, meaning the pacemaker cells themselves fire more slowly.

You don't have to be an athlete to see this effect. Even moderate activity done consistently tends to nudge resting heart rate down. If you're just getting started, [walking every day](/articles/what-happens-when-you-walk-every-day/) is an easy way in.

## What else raises or lowers it

Resting heart rate is not fixed. It responds to what's happening in your body and your day. Mayo Clinic, Cleveland Clinic and Australia's healthdirect service all list a similar set of influences:

**Things that tend to raise it**

- Strong emotions such as stress, anxiety, fear or excitement
- Caffeine, nicotine and alcohol (see [how caffeine works in the brain](/articles/how-does-caffeine-work-in-the-brain/))
- Short or poor-quality sleep
- Fever, infection or pain
- Body position (Mayo Clinic notes that posture can affect your reading)
- An overactive thyroid and some other health conditions
- Lower fitness and higher body weight

**Things that tend to lower it**

- Regular aerobic exercise and higher fitness
- Being asleep or deeply relaxed (heart rate is naturally lower at night)
- Some heart and blood pressure medicines, such as beta blockers

This is why a single high reading after a bad night, a big coffee or a stressful meeting isn't worth worrying about. Patterns matter more than snapshots.

## How to measure your resting heart rate

You can check it without any gadgets:

1. **Pick the right moment.** First thing in the morning, before getting out of bed, is ideal. Otherwise, sit quietly for about 10 minutes.
2. **Find your pulse.** Place your index and middle fingers on the inside of your wrist, just below the base of your thumb. You can also use the side of your neck next to the windpipe, but press gently and only on one side at a time.
3. **Count the beats.** Count for a full 60 seconds, or for 30 seconds and multiply by 2. Counting for 15 seconds and multiplying by 4 is quicker but a little less precise.
4. **Repeat on a few days.** Write down several readings taken under similar conditions and look at the average.

Fitness watches and phone apps can estimate resting heart rate automatically, often while you sleep. They're handy for spotting trends, but Mayo Clinic notes they aren't always accurate, so don't over-interpret small differences.

## When to talk to a health professional

Most variation in resting heart rate is harmless. General guidance from Mayo Clinic and Cleveland Clinic is to check in with a professional if your resting heart rate is regularly above 100, or regularly below 60 when you aren't a trained athlete. It's also worth mentioning if your pulse often feels irregular or noticeably different from your usual pattern.

Seek urgent medical care if a fast or slow heart rate comes with chest pain, fainting, dizziness or shortness of breath.

## The takeaway

A normal resting heart rate for adults is 60 to 100 beats per minute, and fit people are often well below that. Large studies link lower resting rates within the normal range to better long-term health, though the number is shaped by many everyday factors, from sleep and stress to coffee and fever. Measure it the same way on several mornings, watch the trend rather than any single reading, and remember that regular aerobic activity is one of the most dependable ways to bring it down over time.
