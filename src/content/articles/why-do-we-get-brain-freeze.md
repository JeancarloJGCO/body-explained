You take a big, eager bite of ice cream or a long pull on a frozen drink, and a second later a sharp, stabbing pain shoots across your forehead. You squeeze your eyes shut, maybe press a hand to your head, and then, almost as fast as it arrived, it is gone. Brain freeze is one of the strangest little pains the body produces: intense, brief and set off by something as innocent as dessert.

> **In short:** Brain freeze happens when something very cold touches the roof of your mouth or the back of your throat. Blood vessels there react quickly to the sudden cold, and nearby nerves send pain signals that your brain reads as coming from your forehead. It usually lasts less than two minutes and is harmless.

## What actually happens during a brain freeze

Your mouth is packed with blood vessels and nerve endings, especially on the roof of the mouth (the palate) and the back of the throat. These areas are very good at sensing temperature, because your body works hard to keep the head, and the brain inside it, at a steady warmth.

When something icy suddenly presses against these surfaces, a quick chain of events follows. The leading explanation goes like this:

1. **The cold hits.** Ice cream, a frozen drink or even very cold water rapidly chills the palate and throat.
2. **Blood vessels tighten.** Small blood vessels in the area narrow in response to the cold.
3. **Then they rebound.** Moments later, the vessels widen quickly, rushing warm blood back into the area. Cleveland Clinic describes this as the body's attempt to bring warmth back to the head.
4. **Nerves sound the alarm.** Pain receptors pick up this sudden change and send signals along nerve fibers to the **trigeminal nerve**, a large nerve that carries sensation from much of the face and head.
5. **The brain gets the location wrong.** Because the trigeminal nerve also serves the forehead and temples, the brain interprets the pain as a headache rather than as something happening in your mouth.

That last step is called **referred pain**: pain felt in a different place from where the problem starts. It is the same general reason some people feel pain in an arm or jaw when the trouble is somewhere else in the body.

It is worth being honest about the science here. Researchers still debate the exact details, and a 2023 review of the medical literature noted that the mechanism "is still not fully understood." Most experts agree, though, that it involves a combination of cold receptors, blood vessel changes and the trigeminal nerve. The bottom line: your brain does not freeze. The cold never reaches it.

## The surprisingly fancy medical name

Doctors officially call brain freeze a **cold-stimulus headache**. You may also see it called an "ice cream headache," or by the much more impressive name **sphenopalatine ganglioneuralgia**. That name points to a cluster of nerve cells (a ganglion) that sits behind the nose and is linked to the nerves of the palate. You do not need to remember it, but it makes for a good trivia answer.

Headache specialists classify brain freeze as a "primary" headache, which means it is its own thing rather than a symptom of another illness. According to international headache criteria summarized in the 2023 review, a cold-stimulus headache from eating or drinking should clear up within about 10 minutes after the cold is removed. In everyday life, it is usually much shorter than that.

## How common is brain freeze?

Brain freeze is very common, although the numbers vary from study to study.

| Group studied | What researchers found |
|---|---|
| Adults without regular headaches | About 30–40% get brain freeze (Harvard Health) |
| Adults overall, lifetime | Roughly 15–37%, depending on the study (2023 review) |
| 8,359 teenagers in Taiwan | About 41% had experienced it |
| Teens with migraine (same survey) | About 55%, compared with 40% of those without migraine |
| 145 middle-school students in Canada | 79% said they had had it at some point |

A few patterns stand out:

- **Children and teens seem more prone.** Studies of young people often report higher rates than studies of adults.
- **People with migraines get it more often.** Several studies, and sources such as Cleveland Clinic and Harvard Health, note a link between brain freeze and migraine. This connection is one reason headache researchers find brain freeze interesting: it is a safe, short-lived way to study how head pain starts.
- **Family history may play a role.** The 2023 review describes research suggesting that children whose parents get cold-stimulus headaches are more likely to get them too.

## Does eating fast really make it worse?

Yes, and there is a fun experiment to prove it. In 2002, two researchers in Hamilton, Canada, published a study in the *BMJ* in which 145 middle-school students ate a small cup of ice cream either very fast (in under 5 seconds) or slowly (over at least 30 seconds).

The results were clear:

- **27%** of the fast eaters got an ice cream headache.
- **13%** of the slow eaters did.

So gulping your ice cream roughly doubled the chance of brain freeze. Most of the headaches in the study were also very short, with more than half fading in under 10 seconds. It was a small study, but its main finding matches what most people notice in real life.

## How to stop brain freeze fast

Because brain freeze is so brief, it usually goes away on its own before you can do much. Still, these simple steps may help it fade faster, and none of them have any real downside:

1. **Stop eating or drinking the cold thing.** Take it away from your mouth first.
2. **Warm the roof of your mouth.** Press your tongue, curled so the warmer underside faces up, against your palate. Some people press their thumb there instead.
3. **Sip something warm or room temperature.** A drink of lukewarm water can help bring the temperature back up.
4. **Wait it out.** Breathe and give it a few seconds. The pain almost always eases quickly.

Harvard Health points out that there is no proven way to stop brain freeze, so think of these as reasonable tricks rather than sure-fire fixes.

## How to prevent it in the first place

If you are prone to brain freeze, prevention is mostly about slowing down:

- **Eat and drink cold things slowly.** Take small bites and small sips.
- **Let ice cream soften a little** or hold it in the front of your mouth before swallowing, so it warms up before it reaches the palate and throat.
- **Avoid big, sudden temperature changes**, such as chugging a slushie on a hot day.
- **Cover up in very cold weather.** Cold air on your face and head can trigger a similar headache, so a hat or scarf can help.

Some people who get brain freeze often simply start eating frozen treats more carefully. In the large Taiwanese survey, about a third of teens said they had cut back on ice cream because of it, especially younger students.

## When is a headache more than brain freeze?

A single brain freeze is nothing to worry about, and you do not need to see a doctor for one. It is triggered by cold, it is short, and it disappears once the cold is gone.

It is a different story if you have headaches that are frequent, long-lasting or unlike anything you have had before. Cleveland Clinic advises talking to a healthcare professional if you get frequent headaches that last a while. Any severe, sudden headache that does not fade, or a headache that comes with other symptoms such as confusion, weakness or trouble seeing, needs prompt medical attention because it is not a typical brain freeze.

## The takeaway

Brain freeze is your body overreacting, briefly, to a sudden blast of cold on the roof of your mouth or the back of your throat. Blood vessels there tighten and then rebound, nearby nerves fire, and your brain reads the signal as a sharp pain in your forehead. It is harmless and usually gone within seconds. If you want to avoid it, slow down, take smaller bites, and if it strikes anyway, press your tongue to the roof of your mouth and wait it out.
